# How a Compass Points North (Earth's Magnetic Field)

**Alt title:** "Your Compass Is Wrong — And Here's the Physics Why"
**Difficulty:** 2/10 | **Prereq:** None

---

## Opening Hook (0:00–1:00)

The video opens on a top-down view of a canvas showing Earth as a blue circle. Spread across the canvas are hundreds of tiny compass needles, each pointing in a slightly different direction based on its geographic location. Near the equator they point roughly north. Near the poles they swing wildly — some pointing almost straight down, some tilting at odd angles. The host holds up a real compass and says: "This thing is remarkable. No batteries, no GPS, no software — just a magnet that knows where Earth's magnetic pole is. But here's what they don't tell you in school: the magnetic pole is not the North Pole. It wanders around like a drunk tourist, and right now it's in Canada, drifting toward Siberia at about 50 kilometers per year. Let's build the whole field from scratch."

## The Naive Attempt

The viewer starts with the simplest mental model: Earth is a bar magnet aligned with its rotation axis. They implement a perfect dipole field aligned with the geographic north pole. Each compass on the canvas is a small arrow that points in the direction of the horizontal component of the local field vector. The dipole field at position r is:

```js
function dipole(x, y, m) {
  // 2D equatorial slice, m = dipole moment along y
  let r2 = x*x + y*y;
  let r = Math.sqrt(r2);
  let r5 = r2 * r2 * r;
  let Bx = 3*x*(m*0)*1/r5; // simplified 2D dipole
  let By = m / (r*r*r);    // on-axis field
  return { Bx, By };
}
// Place compass needles on a grid:
for (let i = 0; i < rows; i++)
  for (let j = 0; j < cols; j++) {
    let B = dipole(x[j], y[i], M);
    drawArrow(x[j], y[i], Math.atan2(B.By, B.Bx));
  }
```

The field lines form the expected hourglass pattern, and the compass needles make a tidy picture. But the host then overlays a real map of compass declination data and the simulation is visibly wrong in dozens of places.

## The Moment of Failure

The simplified simulation predicts compasses in the United States should point almost perfectly north. But real data shows they can be off by 10–20° east or west depending on where you are. Ships and aircraft have historically run aground because of this error. The sim also says the magnetic pole is right at the geographic north pole — but pulling up the British Geological Survey's pole location puts it in the Canadian Arctic at roughly 86°N, 164°W as of recent years. The dipole-aligned-to-rotation model is fundamentally wrong.

## Why It Broke — The Physics

Earth's magnetic field is generated by the **geodynamo**: convecting liquid iron in the outer core, driven by heat and compositional buoyancy, generates electric currents that sustain the field via self-exciting dynamo action. This process is chaotic and messy. The resulting field is approximately — but not exactly — a dipole, and that dipole is tilted roughly 11° from the rotation axis. On top of the dominant dipole are higher-order multipole terms (quadrupole, octupole) that cause local anomalies. The angle between true geographic north and magnetic north at any location is called **magnetic declination** (δ). On a map this creates isogonic lines — contours of equal declination. Additionally, the field strength and direction change over time (**secular variation**) as the convecting iron currents shift. The north magnetic pole has moved from northern Canada toward Siberia over the past century, accelerating from ~15 km/year to ~50 km/year in recent decades.

## The One Concept

**Earth's magnetic dipole** is the dominant term in a spherical harmonic expansion of the geomagnetic field. The field at the surface can be described by a magnetic potential:

**B = −∇V,  V = (μ₀/4π)(m/r²)·(cosθ)**

where θ is the magnetic colatitude measured from the magnetic pole, r is distance from Earth's center, and m is the dipole moment (~8×10²² A·m²). At any surface point, the field has a horizontal component H pointing toward the magnetic pole and a vertical component Z pointing into the ground in the northern hemisphere (out of the ground in the south). The **dip angle** (or magnetic inclination, I) is `tan(I) = Z/H = 2cosθ/sinθ`; at the magnetic equator I = 0°, at the poles I = ±90°. A compass needle aligns with the horizontal component H. Because the dipole is tilted and Earth's field has non-dipole contributions, every location on Earth has its own unique declination δ — the angle between true north and the direction the compass points. Navigators have always needed to correct for this. The wandering of the pole is evidence that the geodynamo is not a static machine — it evolves over decades, and the historical record in magnetized seafloor rock shows the poles have **reversed** hundreds of times over geologic history, most recently 780,000 years ago.

## The Fix

Add a 11° tilt to the dipole axis and optionally add a small quadrupole perturbation to model non-dipole features:

```js
// Tilted dipole: rotate dipole axis by 11 degrees
let tilt = 11 * Math.PI / 180;
let mx = M * Math.sin(tilt); // dipole moment x-component
let mz = M * Math.cos(tilt); // dipole moment z-component (along tilt axis)
// Compute field at (x, y, z):
let r2 = x*x + y*y + z*z, r = Math.sqrt(r2);
let mdotr = mx*x + mz*z;
let Bx = (3*x*mdotr/r2 - mx) / (r2*r);
let Bz = (3*z*mdotr/r2 - mz) / (r2*r);
```

With the tilt, the magnetic pole on the canvas separates from the geographic pole. The declination map now qualitatively matches the real world — eastern US has westward declination, western US has eastward declination. The host shows the real BGS declination chart beside the simulation for a satisfying comparison.

## The Wow Moment — Push It

Animate the secular variation: show a time-lapse of the magnetic north pole's path from 1900 to 2025, tracing its migration from the Canadian high Arctic toward Siberia. Then demonstrate a **geomagnetic reversal**: slowly rotate the dipole axis by 180°, watching all the compass needles on the map flip as the pole migrates through the equator. During the reversal, show a period of confused, multipole-dominated field with compasses pointing in all directions — this is what actually happens during a reversal, which takes thousands of years.
