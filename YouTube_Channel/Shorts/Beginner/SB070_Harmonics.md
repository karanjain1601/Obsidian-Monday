## Visual Hook (First 3 Seconds)
A guitar string — anchored at both ends — vibrating. The whole string oscillates in one graceful arc: the fundamental mode. Below: a frequency display reading "f₁ = 440 Hz (A4)." Beautiful, clear, simple.

## Main Visual Sequence (0:03–0:45)
**Beat 1 (0:03–0:10):** Fundamental mode: n=1. One loop. Both ends fixed. Wavelength = 2L. Frequency = f₁. String glowing cyan, oscillating slowly and clearly.
**Beat 2 (0:10–0:18):** Second harmonic: n=2. Two loops. One node in the middle. Wavelength = L. Frequency = 2f₁. "SECOND HARMONIC: DOUBLE THE FREQUENCY."
**Beat 3 (0:18–0:26):** Third harmonic: n=3. Three loops. Two nodes. Frequency = 3f₁. Fourth harmonic: four loops, 4f₁. Each appears in succession — animated, glowing.
**Beat 4 (0:26–0:32):** All four modes overlaid simultaneously in different colors. The string appears to vibrate in a complex superposition — which is exactly what happens when you pluck a real guitar string.
**Beat 5 (0:32–0:38):** The spectrum shown: a frequency bar chart with peaks at f₁, 2f₁, 3f₁, 4f₁... "THE HARMONIC SERIES. THE RECIPE OF EVERY MUSICAL SOUND."
**Beat 6 (0:38–0:45):** Touch the string at the midpoint: the fundamental and odd harmonics vanish, only even harmonics remain. "THE GUITARIST PLAYS HARMONICS BY SILENCING NODES." Final: "NODES AND ANTINODES. THE PHYSICS OF MUSIC."

## Physics Concept Teased
A string fixed at both ends can only support standing waves whose wavelengths divide evenly into 2L — producing a harmonic series where each mode is a whole-number multiple of the fundamental frequency, and real string vibration is a superposition of all of them.

## Audio
Each harmonic adds an actual musical pitch: f₁ = A4 (440 Hz). Adding f₂ = A5 (880 Hz). Adding f₃ = E5 (1320 Hz). The progressive addition of harmonics changes the timbre from a pure sine to a rich, complex tone. This is the actual physics of why instruments sound like themselves.
