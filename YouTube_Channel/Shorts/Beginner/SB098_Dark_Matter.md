# Short: Galaxy Rotation Curve

## Visual Hook (First 3 Seconds)
A spiral galaxy rotating — stars near the center moving fast, stars at the edge... also moving fast. That second "also" is the problem. "STARS AT THE EDGE SHOULD BE MOVING SLOWER. THEY'RE NOT."

## Main Visual Sequence (0:03–0:45)
**Beat 1 (0:03–0:10):** What Newtonian gravity predicts for a galaxy: rotation curve shown — rises steeply in the center (dense core), then DROPS as stars get farther from center (less enclosed mass). Like planets in the solar system: inner planets faster, outer planets slower.
**Beat 2 (0:10–0:18):** What is actually observed: the rotation curve rises in the center — then stays nearly flat, all the way to the edge of the visible galaxy. Outer stars: same speed as inner stars. "THE CURVE IS FLAT."
**Beat 3 (0:18–0:26):** The discrepancy: "PREDICTED" (drops off) vs "OBSERVED" (flat). "TO EXPLAIN THE FLAT CURVE: THERE MUST BE MORE MASS." But we can't see it. "WHERE IS THE MISSING MASS?"
**Beat 4 (0:26–0:32):** Dark matter halo: an invisible sphere of mass extending far beyond the visible galaxy. If we add a dark matter halo to the simulation: the rotation curve becomes flat — matching observations. "DARK MATTER HALO."
**Beat 5 (0:32–0:38):** "We can calculate how much dark matter is needed: about 5× the visible matter." Dark matter percentage bar: "VISIBLE: 15%. DARK MATTER: 85%." "WE CAN'T SEE 85% OF THE GALAXY."
**Beat 6 (0:38–0:45):** "We don't know what dark matter is. We know it's there because of its gravity." Final: "WHERE IS THE MISSING MASS? 60 YEARS LATER: STILL UNKNOWN."

## Physics Concept Teased
Galaxy rotation curves — stars orbiting at the same speed regardless of distance from the center — cannot be explained by visible matter alone. Dark matter, an invisible mass component extending far beyond the visible disk, is required to flatten the rotation curve.
