# Short: Two Pendulums, One Lie

## Visual Hook (First 3 Seconds)
Two identical pendulums hang side by side. Both are pulled to exactly 60° — angle markers are shown. Text: "SAME START. SAME CODE." They release simultaneously, sweeping in perfect sync. Satisfying, rhythmic.

## Main Visual Sequence (0:03–0:50)
**Beat 1 (0:03–0:12):** Swings 1 and 2. Perfect synchrony. The two pendulums overlap exactly. "IN SYNC." appears.
**Beat 2 (0:12–0:22):** Swing 3. A tiny gap opens — barely visible. Swing 4. The gap is undeniable. One is ahead of the other by a fraction of a degree at the peak.
**Beat 3 (0:22–0:30):** Swing 5. One pendulum is going LEFT while the other is going RIGHT. They've inverted. The phase difference is now 180°. Freeze frame. "HOW?"
**Beat 4 (0:30–0:38):** The two traces are overlaid in different colors — cyan and orange. The divergence is drawn as a growing gap that widens swing by swing. "THEY STARTED IDENTICAL."
**Beat 5 (0:38–0:44):** Labels appear on each: "SMALL-ANGLE APPROX." (one) and "EXACT ODE" (other). The small-angle one is running ahead because sin θ ≈ θ overestimates restoring force at large angles.
**Beat 6 (0:44–0:50):** Final text: "At 60°, the approximation is 12% wrong. By swing 5, it's completely wrong. ONE OF THESE IS LYING."

## Physics Concept Teased
At angles beyond ~15°, the small-angle approximation sin θ ≈ θ introduces accumulated error with every swing — but how quickly does that error make the simulation completely wrong?
