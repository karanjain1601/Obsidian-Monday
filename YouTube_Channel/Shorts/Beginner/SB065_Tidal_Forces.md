## Visual Hook (First 3 Seconds)
Earth and Moon. Ocean shown as a blue layer around Earth. Most people expect one bulge toward the Moon. Instead: TWO bulges appear — one toward the Moon, one away from it. "WHY TWO?"

## Main Visual Sequence (0:03–0:45)
**Beat 1 (0:03–0:10):** "Wrong intuition" shown first: one bulge toward the Moon. "WHAT MOST PEOPLE EXPECT." Then reset.
**Beat 2 (0:10–0:18):** The correct picture: gravity vectors shown at three points on Earth. Near-Moon side: strong pull (toward Moon). Earth center: medium pull. Far side: weaker pull. "DIFFERENTIAL GRAVITY."
**Beat 3 (0:18–0:26):** In the Earth's reference frame: near side is pulled MORE than center (net: toward Moon). Far side is pulled LESS than center (net: AWAY from Moon). Both sides bulge outward relative to Earth's center. "TWO DIFFERENTIAL PULLS."
**Beat 4 (0:26–0:32):** The two bulges shown forming: one toward the Moon (near-side pull) and one away from the Moon (far-side lag). Both caused by the same gravitational differential.
**Beat 5 (0:32–0:38):** Earth rotating under the two bulges: as Earth spins, a coastal city passes through both bulges — two high tides per day, roughly 12 hours apart. "TWO HIGHS. TWO LOWS. EVERY DAY."
**Beat 6 (0:38–0:45):** "It's not the Moon pulling the ocean toward it. It's the Moon pulling DIFFERENTLY across the diameter of Earth." Final: "TIDAL FORCES. DIFFERENTIAL GRAVITY."

## Physics Concept Teased
The Moon doesn't simply pull Earth's ocean toward it — it pulls the near side more than Earth's center, and Earth's center more than the far side. This differential creates two tidal bulges from a single gravitational source.
