# Short: The Moment It Slides

## Visual Hook (First 3 Seconds)
A wooden block sitting on a ramp. The ramp is tilting slowly, degree by degree. The block stays still. 10°. 15°. 20°. 25°. Still stationary. Then — at exactly one more degree — it slides.

## Main Visual Sequence (0:03–0:40)
**Beat 1 (0:03–0:10):** Ramp tilting. Angle counter shown: 5°, 10°, 15°, 20°. Block stationary. Friction force arrows shown (opposing the gravity component along the slope) — friction is exactly matching gravity. "STATIC FRICTION."
**Beat 2 (0:10–0:18):** At 25°: friction arrow and gravity-slope arrow equal. "AT THE LIMIT." Friction force is at maximum static value: f_s = μ_s × N. One more degree: something has to give.
**Beat 3 (0:18–0:24):** At 26°: the block suddenly starts sliding. Friction arrow instantly changes: "KINETIC FRICTION" label — and it's shorter than the static friction was. Less resistance once sliding begins. Block accelerates.
**Beat 4 (0:24–0:32):** "WHY LESS FRICTION WHEN SLIDING?" Microscopic view: surface irregularities and asperities. Static: locked together. Kinetic: rolling and sliding over each other — less energy loss. "μ_k < μ_s. ALWAYS."
**Beat 5 (0:32–0:37):** The block sliding down. Kinetic friction constant regardless of speed (for this model). "μ_s = 0.45. μ_k = 0.35."
**Beat 6 (0:37–0:40):** "One degree. The difference between staying and sliding. Between static and kinetic friction." Final: "THE THRESHOLD."

## Physics Concept Teased
Static friction can grow to match any applied force up to its maximum (μ_s × N), but once exceeded, it instantly drops to the smaller kinetic value (μ_k × N) — meaning objects resist sliding but then suddenly slide easier once started.
