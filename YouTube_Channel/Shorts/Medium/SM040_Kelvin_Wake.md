# Short: Kelvin Wake — Always 19.47°

**Feeds full video:** Standalone medium short

## Visual Hook (First 3 Seconds)
Satellite view (or clean simulation): a ship moving across a dark ocean. Behind it a perfect V-shaped wake — two straight lines of white foam at 19.47° from the ship's path — regardless of speed. The angle is labeled in bold yellow text.

## Main Visual Sequence (0:03–0:50)
**0:03–0:10** — Mathematical derivation visualised: a point source moving at speed U emits circular waves at each moment. The wave phase velocity c_p(k) = √(g/k) varies with wavenumber. The stationary phase condition: the envelope of all circular waves forms the Kelvin wedge. Caption: "All speeds → same 19.47° angle."

**0:10–0:18** — Speed variation: the ship travels at 10 knots, 20 knots, 30 knots. The wake pattern looks visually different (more waves inside the wedge at higher speed) but the outer wedge angle remains exactly 19.47° = arcsin(1/3). Caption: "sin(θ) = 1/3 exactly."

**0:18–0:27** — Inside the wedge: two types of waves visible — transverse waves (perpendicular to ship's path, parallel crests) and divergent waves (angled away from the path). The cusp where they meet is the 19.47° line. Annotation: "Transverse + divergent waves inside the wedge."

**0:27–0:36** — The universality: the Kelvin angle depends only on gravity and the dispersion relation ω² = gk. It is the same on any planet with any gravity, any ship speed. Caption: "It would be 19.47° on Mars too." The formula: sin(θ_K) = 1/3 independent of g and U.

**0:36–0:45** — Shallow water exception: if water depth h is small (kh < 1), the dispersion relation changes and the Kelvin angle can be larger. Caption: "Shallow water: angle > 19.47°." Bold text: "Kelvin wake — one of the most elegant results in wave physics." Fade to black.

## Physics Concept Teased
Kelvin wake: the envelope of all waves generated by a moving ship on deep water always subtends the same half-angle — 19.47° (= arcsin(1/3)) — regardless of ship speed. This universal result follows from the deep-water dispersion relation ω² = gk via a stationary phase argument (Lord Kelvin, 1887).

## Production Notes
Code complexity: moderate. Renderer: Canvas 2D (top-down view). Key algorithm: superpose circular wave pulses emitted at regular time intervals along the ship's path. Height field: h(x,t) = Σ_i A(t-t_i) cos(k|r-r_i| - ω(k)(t-t_i)) where r_i is the emission position, summed over k (dispersion). For the clean envelope: use stationary phase approximation analytically — compute and draw the Kelvin angle as geometric construction. Ship path drawn as a moving white dot leaving a trail. Gotcha: displaying many wave circles simultaneously is computationally expensive — pre-render the wave pattern. Runtime: pre-rendered wave pattern, real-time ship movement overlay.
