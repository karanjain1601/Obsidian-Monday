# People Management

> [!abstract] TL;DR
> People management is the operational core of engineering leadership: weekly 1:1s, SBI feedback, performance conversations, career laddering, promotion advocacy, and managing up. Done well, it builds trust, accelerates growth, and retains talent. Done poorly, it produces surprise attrition and surprise PIPs.

## 1:1 Structure and Cadence

### The Weekly 1:1
- **Frequency:** Weekly, 30–50 minutes. Never skip unless both parties agree to a specific reschedule.
- **Agenda ownership:** The report owns the agenda. This is not the manager's meeting — it is theirs.
- **Status updates belong elsewhere:** If your 1:1 has become a standup, that is a relationship signal, not a scheduling fix.
- **EM's standing prep:** Review prior action items, check for blockers flagged in tickets, note any behavioral or performance signals to address.

### 1:1 Agenda Template

```
--- Report's Agenda ---
(populated by the engineer before the meeting)
1.
2.
3.

--- EM's Standing Agenda Items ---
(added only as needed)
- Career / growth check-in (monthly rotation)
- Feedback I want to share
- Anything I can unblock or remove from your plate?

--- Follow-Up from Last Week ---
Action items:
-
```

### 1:1 Conversation Types and Frequency

| Type | Cadence | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Tactical / unblocking | Every week | Current work, immediate blockers, quick context |
| Career growth | Monthly | Skills gaps, aspirations, next-level behaviors |
| Relationship / trust | Quarterly | How the working relationship feels, what EM can do better |
| Performance feedback | As needed | Specific behavioral feedback using SBI |
| Big picture | Quarterly | Team direction, company context, how engineer's work connects |

## The SBI Feedback Model

**Situation — Behavior — Impact**. The most actionable framework for delivering specific, defensible feedback that avoids judgment and enables behavior change.

| Component | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | When and where did this happen? | "In yesterday's architecture review..." |
| **Behavior** | What observable action occurred? (Facts, not interpretation) | "...you interrupted Priya twice before she finished her point..." |
| **Impact** | What was the measurable or perceived effect? | "...and I noticed the room went quiet. I'm concerned it's signaling to others that interrupting is acceptable here." |

### SBI Anti-Patterns

| Anti-pattern | Problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Skipping Situation | Feedback feels vague or chronic | Always anchor to a specific event |
| Describing interpretation, not behavior | "You were aggressive" is unfalsifiable | "You raised your voice twice" is observable |
| Skipping Impact | Feedback feels personal, not professional | Tie behavior to team or product outcome |
| "Feedback sandwich" | Dilutes the message; report remembers only the compliment | Deliver feedback cleanly; follow up separately with positive recognition |

### SBI for Positive Feedback
SBI works equally well for reinforcing behaviors you want repeated: "In the P1 incident last Thursday [S], you kept the team calm and delegated diagnostics clearly [B], which meant we resolved in 55 minutes instead of our typical two-hour window [I]. That is exactly the kind of leadership I want to see you apply more broadly."

## Performance Conversations and PIPs

### The Feedback Escalation Ladder

1. **Ongoing SBI feedback in 1:1s** — Delivered within 24–48 hours of the behavior
2. **Named pattern conversation** — "I want to name a pattern I'm seeing across several incidents..."
3. **Written informal warning** — Documented in 1:1 notes; shared with HR for record
4. **Performance Improvement Plan (PIP)** — Formal document with timeline, expectations, and consequences

> A PIP should never surprise the engineer. If it does, earlier feedback was not delivered clearly or frequently enough.

### PIP Structure Template

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| **Current State** | 3–5 specific, SBI-documented behaviors and their impact |
| **Expected Behaviors** | Concrete, observable, and measurable outcomes required |
| **Timeline** | 30/60/90-day plan with check-in dates |
| **Support Offered** | Coaching sessions, resources, reduced scope during recovery |
| **Consequence Clause** | Clear statement of what occurs if expectations are not met |

## Career Ladders and Leveling

### Standard IC Leveling (E3–E7 / L3–L7 Style)

| Level | Scope | Autonomy Signal |
|---|---|---|
| E3 (Junior) | Task | Needs guidance; produces correct output with direction |
| E4 (Mid) | Feature | Executes independently within a defined project |
| E5 (Senior) | Project | Delivers end-to-end; mentors E3/E4; technical decisions within team |
| E6 (Staff) | Team / Org | Drives technical strategy across multiple teams; sets direction |
| E7 (Principal) | Company | Shapes major technical investments; organizational influence |

### Staff+ Levels
Beyond E7, levels (Distinguished, Fellow) operate at business-unit or company scope. Their impact is measured in years, not quarters. See [[Staff_Plus_Engineering]] for detail.

### The "Already Operating" Rule
Promotions should confirm ongoing behavior — not create new expectations. Advocate for promotion when:
- The engineer has been **demonstrably operating at the next level** for at least 2 consecutive quarters
- You have **documented evidence** of impact at that level (not just potential)
- Peers would not be surprised — the promotion would feel overdue, not premature

## Promotion Advocacy

### Building the Promotion Case

1. Start collecting evidence 2–3 quarters before the cycle
2. Write the promo packet: impact summary, level rubric mapping, peer signals, manager narrative
3. Identify "comparable profiles" — engineers recently promoted at the target level whose impact is similar
4. Present at calibration with specifics; anecdote-free advocacy rarely lands

### Sponsorship vs. Mentorship

| Dimension | Mentorship | Sponsorship |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Advice, feedback, guidance | Active advocacy and opportunity creation |
| Venue | 1:1 conversations | Staff meetings, hiring panels, promo calibrations |
| Risk to provider | Minimal | Sponsor's reputation is on the line |
| Impact on recipient | Incremental skill growth | Career-defining visibility and trajectory |

Great EMs do both. The most powerful thing an EM can do for a high-performing engineer is say their name — positively, specifically — in rooms the engineer is not in. This is sponsorship.

## Team Morale Signals

Watch for these early-warning indicators before a resignation letter appears:

| Signal | Possible Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1:1s becoming shorter / quieter | Loss of trust or engagement |
| Decrease in PR comments submitted | Withdrawal from team culture |
| Repeated "I'm fine" when asked directly | Psychological safety has eroded |
| Engineer stops volunteering for new work | Lack of investment in the team's future |
| Sudden uptick in PTO requests | Interviewing elsewhere |

### The Stay Interview
Run a "stay interview" annually with every direct report: "What would make you want to leave? What would make you stay?" Most managers only discover the answers at the exit interview — too late.

## Managing Up

**Core principle:** Your manager should never be surprised by bad news. Proactive, honest communication upward is the foundation of credibility.

### The Weekly Written Update Template
```
Team Update — Week of [date]
Wins: [2–3 bullets on progress made]
Blockers: [Issues outside team control that need leadership support]
Risks: [Things that might go wrong; early signals]
FYI: [Context leadership should have even if no action needed]
```

### Managing Up Checklist
- [ ] Weekly: Send brief written update — wins, blockers, risks
- [ ] Monthly: Share team health signal (morale indicators, attrition risk, capacity)
- [ ] Quarterly: Align on team OKRs and roadmap priorities with skip-level
- [ ] Immediately: Escalate anything outside your control to resolve within 24 hours

### Escalation Frame
"Here is the situation. Here is what I have already tried. Here is the specific decision or resource I need from you." Never bring a problem without having attempted a resolution.

## Common Pitfalls

- Letting performance issues accumulate to avoid a difficult conversation — problems grow, they do not self-resolve
- Running 1:1s as status updates — this signals the relationship is primarily transactional
- Giving only positive feedback in 1:1s and saving critical feedback for annual reviews — causes surprise and resentment
- Advocating only for engineers who remind you of yourself — conscious inclusion in sponsorship is an equity obligation
- Writing a PIP as the first feedback a report has ever received — PIPs must be the last step, not the first

## Review Questions

1. Write an SBI feedback message for this scenario: "An engineer repeatedly misses the daily standup without prior notice and without catching up async."
2. You have an E5 engineer who is frustrated they have not been promoted to E6 after three years at E5. What would you cover in a career growth 1:1, and what evidence would you need to make a case for promotion?
3. What is the distinction between sponsorship and mentorship, and why does it matter specifically for engineers from underrepresented groups?
4. A direct report tells you during a 1:1 that they feel burned out. Using the morale signals framework, what would you look for retrospectively, and what do you do next?
