# Logical Fallacies Overview

> [!abstract] TL;DR
> A logical fallacy is a defect in reasoning that makes an argument unreliable — either because the logical form is structurally broken regardless of content (formal fallacy) or because premises mislead through irrelevance, hidden assumptions, or linguistic ambiguity despite a superficially valid structure (informal fallacy). The four major families of informal fallacy — relevance, presumption, ambiguity, and insufficient evidence — cover virtually every rhetorical trap encountered in real debate and political discourse. Recognizing a fallacy is a starting point for analysis, not a refutation: labeling "slippery slope" and walking away is itself a rhetorical maneuver that substitutes a name for actual scrutiny.

---

## Intuition

**Analogy:** A structural engineer inspecting a bridge does not condemn it because it looks shaky or because she dislikes the architect. She works through a checklist: Are the load-bearing members the right grade of steel? Does the span account for thermal expansion? Are the joints correctly welded? A *structural* defect — wrong-grade steel throughout — condemns the bridge regardless of how it looks; the blueprint was wrong from the start. A *material* defect — correct blueprint, but corroded bolts substituted in one junction — requires examining the specific content of that junction, not just the form.

Formal fallacies are structural defects: the logical blueprint guarantees unreliability no matter what the premises say. Informal fallacies are material defects: the form appears sound, but the actual content of the premises — their relevance, their hidden assumptions, their shifting meanings — fails under scrutiny. Fallacy identification is the engineer's checklist for arguments: its goal is not to demolish every bridge in sight, but to determine precisely which ones are safe to cross and which will collapse under weight.

## How It Works

### Core Mechanics

The word *fallacy* derives from Latin *fallacia* — deception, trick. This etymology is apt: the defining feature of a fallacy is not merely that it produces a false conclusion but that it does so through a form of reasoning that *feels convincing* while being unreliable. An argument that is obviously wrong is not a fallacy in the interesting sense; a fallacy is a trap that catches trained minds.

**Two top-level divisions:**

**1. Formal fallacies** are errors in the logical *form* of an argument. Because validity is a purely structural property (it holds across all possible substitutions of content into the form), a formal fallacy is detectable without knowing whether any premise is actually true. The most common:

- **Affirming the Consequent** — *If P then Q; Q; therefore P.* Invalid because Q may be true for many reasons besides P. "If the server is down, the website is unreachable. The website is unreachable. Therefore the server is down" — but a DNS failure, routing issue, or client-side block could equally cause it.
- **Denying the Antecedent** — *If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q.* Invalid because a different cause could still produce Q. "If you study hard, you will pass. You did not study hard. Therefore you will not pass" — but you might pass through luck or prior mastery.
- **Undistributed Middle** — In a syllogism, the middle term must be distributed (referring to all members of its class) in at least one premise. "All cats are mammals; all dogs are mammals; therefore all cats are dogs" — *mammals* appears in both premises but covers different subsets in each.

**2. Informal fallacies** cannot be detected from logical form alone; diagnosing them requires examining the *meaning, context, and use* of the argument. They subdivide into four major families:

**Fallacies of Relevance** — The premises are psychologically compelling but logically disconnected from the conclusion. The emotional or social force of the premises does the persuasive work that the logical relationship should do.

| Fallacy | Mechanism | Classic Instance |
|---------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Ad Hominem** | Attacks the arguer rather than the argument | "You cannot trust her climate analysis — she once exaggerated on her CV" |
| **Straw Man** | Misrepresents the opponent's position to defeat an easier target | "You want to regulate guns? So you want to leave citizens defenseless" |
| **Appeal to Authority** | Uses an expert's status as a substitute for evidence; defective when the authority is unqualified, biased, or outside their domain | "A Nobel laureate says vaccines cause autism" (outside their domain) |
| **Appeal to Emotion** | Uses pity, fear, flattery, or outrage to bypass rational evaluation | "Think of the children — you must vote for this bill" |
| **Red Herring** | Introduces an irrelevant issue to distract from the actual point | A politician asked about policy failures pivots to national pride |
| **Appeal to the Crowd** | Treats popular acceptance as evidence of truth | "Millions believe it, so it must be true" |

**Fallacies of Presumption** — The argument's premises secretly assume what needs to be proved, or rely on false background assumptions the audience accepts without scrutiny.

| Fallacy | Mechanism | Classic Instance |
|---------|-----------|-----------------|
| **False Dilemma** | Presents two options as exhaustive when others exist | "You are either with us or against us" |
| **Begging the Question** | The conclusion is hidden inside a premise | "The Bible is true because God wrote it, and we know God wrote it because the Bible says so" |
| **Hasty Generalization** | Draws a universal conclusion from an unrepresentatively small sample | "I met two rude Parisians; the French are rude" |
| **Slippery Slope** | Claims one step inevitably leads to an extreme outcome without establishing the causal mechanism | "Legalizing marijuana will inevitably lead to heroin addiction" |
| **False Cause / Post Hoc** | Infers causation from mere temporal succession | "I wore my lucky socks; we won; therefore my socks caused the win" |
| **Complex Question** | Embeds a contested assumption in a question | "Have you stopped cheating on exams?" (presupposes prior cheating) |

**Fallacies of Ambiguity** — The argument exploits the multiple meanings of words or phrases, shifting senses between premises and conclusion. The logical move appears valid only because a key term is used in two different senses.

| Fallacy | Mechanism |
|---------|-----------|
| **Equivocation** | A key term shifts meaning between premises: "Laws of nature cannot be broken; laws against theft are laws; therefore theft cannot be committed" — *law* means physical regularity in premise 1, legal statute in premise 2 |
| **Amphiboly** | Grammatical ambiguity in a sentence: "We will sell you nothing too good for your money" — are we selling you excellent goods for your money, or are we selling you nothing? |
| **Accent** | Meaning shifts depending on which word receives emphasis: "We should *not* speak ill of our friends" vs. "We should not speak ill of *our* friends" (implying others are fair game) |
| **Composition** | Attributes to a whole what is true only of its parts: "Every player on this team is excellent; therefore the team is excellent" — but excellent players may have poor chemistry |
| **Division** | Attributes to the parts what is true only of the whole: "This is an excellent team; therefore every player on it is excellent" |

**Fallacies of Insufficient Evidence** — The conclusion is asserted with more confidence than the evidence warrants. The argument form may be valid but the evidentiary support is structurally inadequate.

| Fallacy | Mechanism |
|---------|-----------|
| **Appeal to Ignorance** | Treats absence of evidence as evidence of absence or presence: "No one has proved ghosts don't exist; therefore they do" |
| **Weak Analogy** | Two things are analogous in some respects but the relevant respects are disanalogous |
| **Biased Sample / Hasty Generalization** | The sample from which a generalization is drawn is unrepresentative |
| **Gambler's Fallacy** | Believes past independent events affect the probability of future ones: "The coin has landed heads ten times; tails is overdue" |

### Historical Roots: Aristotle's De Sophisticis Elenchis

Aristotle's *De Sophisticis Elenchis* (On Sophistical Refutations, c. 350 BCE) is the first systematic catalogue of fallacies. Aristotle divided thirteen fallacies into two groups:

**In dictione** (dependent on language, six fallacies): equivocation, amphiboly, combination, division, accent, and form of expression. These exploit the surface features of language — its ambiguity, its grammatical structure — to create the illusion of valid inference.

**Extra dictionem** (independent of language, seven fallacies): accident, *secundum quid* (hasty generalization ignoring qualifications), ignoratio elenchi (proving the wrong conclusion), petitio principii (begging the question), non causa pro causa (false cause), consequent (affirming the consequent), and many questions (complex question). These do not depend on linguistic accidents but on substantive errors in how evidence and conclusions are connected.

Aristotle's context was the Sophists — professional teachers of rhetoric who trained clients to win arguments regardless of truth. His goal was to expose the exact mechanisms by which apparently valid refutations are actually defective, so that both practitioners and audiences could recognize the tricks. Every modern fallacy catalogue descends from this list.

### The Pragma-Dialectical Account

Van Eemeren and Grootendorst's pragma-dialectics reconceives fallacies not as a catalogue of rhetorical tricks but as violations of the rules for *critical discussion* — the normative procedure by which rational agents resolve genuine disagreements. On this account, every fallacy violates a specific rule, which explains *why* it is wrong rather than simply labeling it suspicious:

- **Ad hominem** violates Rule 1 (Freedom Rule): parties must be allowed to advance and test standpoints without personal interference.
- **Straw man** violates Rule 3 (Standpoint Rule): attacks must address the actual standpoint, not a misrepresentation.
- **Appeal to Authority** (misapplied) violates Rule 7 (Validity Rule): only genuinely valid argumentation schemes may be used.
- **Begging the Question** violates Rule 6 (Starting Points Rule): premises must be genuinely shared starting points, not covert restatements of the conclusion.
- **Slippery Slope** (unfounded) violates Rule 7 when the causal chain linking steps is asserted without evidence.

This framework has a critical implication: most informal fallacy patterns are *not automatically fallacious*. An appeal to authority is legitimate when the authority is genuine, unbiased, and in-domain, and when their claim is consistent with other expert opinion. A slippery-slope argument is legitimate when a real and well-evidenced causal mechanism connects the steps. The pragma-dialectical question is always: does this instance of the pattern violate the applicable rule, or does it satisfy the *critical questions* that make the pattern legitimate?

### Fallacy Labeling as a Rhetorical Weapon

One of the most important meta-level insights in modern fallacy theory is that fallacy labels can themselves be deployed fallaciously. The *ad hominem* of labeling — dismissing an argument by attaching a fallacy name to it rather than identifying the specific logical defect — is sometimes called the *ad lapidem* move: "the stone" (throwing the stone of a label without engaging the argument's content).

The pattern is common in public discourse: a commentator labels an opponent's argument "slippery slope" or "false dilemma" without explaining which specific causal mechanism is missing, or which genuine third option was excluded. This exploits the rhetorical authority that fallacy terminology has accumulated — it sounds analytical while doing no analysis. The correct response to any argument suspected of a fallacy is to identify which specific logical rule is violated and why, then invite the original arguer to repair or defend their reasoning. The goal of fallacy identification in everyday life is not winning but understanding: exposing exactly where the reasoning went wrong and giving it the opportunity to be corrected.

## Key Concepts

### Secondary

**What is a fallacy?** A fallacy is a pattern of reasoning that feels compelling but fails to reliably support its conclusion. The key word is *pattern* — fallacies are not random errors but systematic defects that recur across different topics and contexts. Aristotle identified them as *sophistical refutations*: arguments that have the appearance of valid refutation but are actually deceptive.

**Formal vs. informal:** The clearest way to distinguish them is by the diagnostic method. A formal fallacy can be identified by looking only at the argument's form — the arrangement of logical connectives and variables — without knowing what the subject matter is. An informal fallacy requires reading the actual content, understanding the context, and asking whether the premises actually do the logical work they appear to do.

**Why fallacies feel convincing:** This is the psychologically interesting question. Ad hominem works because character *is* evidence of reliability in everyday social life — we do not usually trust information from people we know to be dishonest. It becomes fallacious in argument contexts because the question is whether the argument's *premises* are true and its *form* is valid, not whether the arguer is trustworthy. The same psychological heuristic (distrust unreliable sources) that serves us well in casual conversation misleads us when evaluating formal arguments. This is the deep connection between fallacy theory and cognitive bias research.

**The basic identification method:**
1. Reconstruct the argument in standard form (explicit premises, stated conclusion).
2. Ask: does the conclusion follow from these premises, or is there a gap?
3. If there is a gap, identify whether it is a structural gap (formal fallacy) or a content gap (informal fallacy).
4. Name the specific defect, explain which logical rule it violates, and explain what a repaired version of the argument would need to say.

### Undergraduate

**Aristotle's thirteen fallacies in De Sophisticis Elenchis** represent the founding document of informal logic. His distinction between *in dictione* (language-dependent) and *extra dictionem* (language-independent) fallacies establishes the basic insight that not all argumentative defects are the same kind of thing. The *in dictione* fallacies anticipate modern philosophy of language: equivocation is the failure to maintain type-identity for a term across an argument; amphiboly is the exploitation of syntactic ambiguity in natural language; accent demonstrates that meaning is not only lexical but prosodic and pragmatic.

The *extra dictionem* fallacies are substantive rather than linguistic. *Ignoratio elenchi* (proving an irrelevant conclusion) is Aristotle's most general irrelevance fallacy — it encompasses anything that proves something other than what was at issue. *Petitio principii* (begging the question) is routinely misunderstood in contemporary usage: the phrase now colloquially means "raises the question," but in Aristotle and the logical tradition it means *assuming the conclusion as a premise*, a circular argument structure that cannot do genuine evidential work because its support is self-generated.

**The four informal fallacy families** map onto distinct violations of rational discourse:

*Relevance fallacies* are emotional or social substitutes for logical connection. They work by importing a true but logically irrelevant premise — the arguer really is dishonest, the audience really should feel compassion — and leveraging its psychological weight to do the inferential work that an evidential connection should do.

*Presumption fallacies* work by hiding work. The false dilemma presupposes that only two options exist; begging the question presupposes the conclusion in a premise worded differently enough to obscure the circularity; the complex question presupposes the truth of an embedded claim before the questioner has the right to assume it. In each case, a contested assumption travels through the argument in disguise.

*Ambiguity fallacies* exploit the gap between *type* and *token* for linguistic expressions. Equivocation is particularly common in analogical arguments: "The laws of nature are inviolable; the law against murder is a law; therefore the law against murder is inviolable" — the word *law* covers two radically different concepts. The argument's validity is purchased by conflating them.

*Insufficient evidence fallacies* are fallacies of degree rather than kind. The appeal to ignorance is not always fallacious — in legal contexts, presumption of innocence is a principled assignment of the burden of proof that makes the state prove guilt rather than the defendant prove innocence. The fallacy arises when absence of evidence is used positively to establish a conclusion in contexts where the evidence should be findable if the conclusion were true.

**Charles Hamblin's critique** (*Fallacies*, 1970) is a landmark intervention in fallacy theory. Hamblin showed that the *standard treatment* of fallacies — the catalogue-based presentation found in logic textbooks — was intellectually incoherent: it never defined what a fallacy was, it presented examples without principled criteria for fallacy-hood, and it tacitly assumed that fallacies are obvious to detect in practice while being structurally interesting in theory. Hamblin's diagnosis opened the door to the pragma-dialectical approach: if fallacies are not simply formal errors, they must be defined normatively, relative to the rules of rational discourse. This is exactly what van Eemeren and Grootendorst then provided.

**Douglas Walton's argumentation schemes** reframe the relationship between fallacies and legitimate argument. Walton, Reed, and Macagno catalogued over sixty *argumentation schemes* — defeasible inference patterns common in everyday and legal reasoning — each accompanied by a set of *critical questions* that determine whether the scheme is being used properly. The appeal to authority is an argumentation scheme with critical questions: Is the source genuinely expert? Is the claim within their domain? Do they have a conflict of interest? Is the claim consistent with other expert opinion? When the critical questions are satisfactorily answered, the appeal to authority is *not* a fallacy — it is legitimate defeasible reasoning. When the critical questions are not answered, the fallacy diagnosis is more specific: which critical question was violated? This makes fallacy analysis a tool for productive dialogue rather than a conversation-stopper.

### Graduate

**The paradox of the fallacy:** If a fallacy is by definition a defective argument, why does it persuade? The philosophical puzzle is that fallacies cannot be explained purely by logical theory, which would predict that invalid arguments fail to persuade. They require a theory of *bounded rationality* — agents with limited time, limited working memory, motivated reasoning, and evolutionary heuristics operating in environments where the heuristics misfire. The deep theoretical claim is that fallacies are not errors in an alien logic; they are the by-products of *good* heuristics running on problems they were not designed for. This is the connection to dual-process theory (Kahneman's System 1 vs. System 2): fallacies exploit the fast, associative, affect-driven System 1 by making the premises emotionally or socially salient in ways that substitute for the slow, analytic evaluation of System 2.

**Epistemic vs. dialectical accounts of fallacy:** Two competing frameworks exist for what makes a fallacy wrong. The *epistemic* account (Siegel, Johnson) holds that fallacies are wrong because they produce beliefs unreliably — they are epistemically irresponsible moves that violate the norms of good belief formation. The *dialectical* account (van Eemeren, Walton) holds that fallacies are wrong because they violate the norms of rational discourse — they are procedural violations of the rules that make joint inquiry possible. The two accounts make different predictions: the epistemic account entails that a fallacy is wrong even in a monologue context where no dialogue is occurring; the dialectical account entails that a fallacy is defined only relative to the dialogue context in which it occurs. Most contemporary theorists hold a pluralist position: different fallacies are best analyzed by different frameworks, and both epistemic and dialectical norms are relevant in different contexts.

**The indexicality of fallacy:** Several fallacy types are genuinely context-sensitive in ways that make categorical labeling misleading. Whether an appeal to authority is fallacious depends entirely on the authority's credentials, biases, and domain relevance. Whether a slippery slope is fallacious depends on whether the causal mechanism linking the steps is real. Whether an ad hominem is fallacious depends on whether the personal fact invoked is genuinely relevant to argument credibility (circumstantial ad hominem probing conflict of interest) or genuinely irrelevant (abusive ad hominem substituting character attack for content engagement). The theoretical implication is that *no* informal fallacy pattern is categorically fallacious; each requires case-by-case evaluation of whether the critical conditions for legitimate use are met. This is epistemically demanding but practically important: it prevents fallacy labeling from becoming a thought-terminating cliche.

**Formal treatment via relevance logic:** Classical logic is *explosive*: from a contradiction, any conclusion follows (*ex falso quodlibet*). This makes it impossible to represent the difference between a valid-but-unreliable argument and an invalid argument in classical logic. Relevance logic (Anderson and Belnap, 1975) adds a *relevance condition* to the classical truth-preservation account of validity: for an argument to be valid, the premises must be *relevant* to the conclusion in a precise sense — the conclusion must use the informational content of each premise. Relevance logic can therefore formally represent the difference between an argument that is truth-preserving but irrelevant (a fallacy of relevance in the classical sense) and an argument that is both truth-preserving and evidentially connected. This gives formal fallacy theory a richer semantic foundation than classical propositional logic alone provides.

## Real-World Applications

> **Political Debate — Straw Man and False Dilemma as Standard Repertoire.** Legislative debates and electoral campaigns systematically deploy straw man constructions: a politician proposing modest gun-safety legislation is characterized as "wanting to confiscate all firearms," and a candidate for stricter environmental regulation is described as "wanting to destroy the economy." The false dilemma reinforces the construction: "Either you support total gun rights or you support total government disarmament" excludes the actual middle positions proposed. These are not accidental rhetoric — they are deliberate strategy, tested by focus groups and calibrated to exploit the cognitive architecture of rapid emotional evaluation. Critical discourse analysis (Fairclough) and pragma-dialectics both identify these as Rule 3 violations (standpoint rule), but remediation requires that the violated rule be named publicly, which demands an audience equipped with fallacy literacy.

> **Legal Reasoning — Post Hoc Fallacy in Causation Disputes.** Product liability and tort law turn on the distinction between correlation and causation — the conceptual core of the post hoc ergo propter hoc fallacy. A plaintiff's argument that a medication caused their adverse outcome because the outcome followed use of the medication must survive a causation analysis that asks whether temporally prior presence of the drug could have been incidental. Courts require the "but-for" test and Daubert standards for expert testimony precisely to filter post-hoc reasoning: the expert must establish a plausible biological mechanism, not merely a temporal sequence. The legal system has thus institutionalized the informal-logic insight that succession does not entail causation, building the anti-fallacy standard into procedural law.

> **Scientific Communication — Appeal to Authority and Vaccine Hesitancy.** A significant vector of vaccine hesitancy is the misapplied appeal to authority: celebrity testimonials, in-domain-adjacent professionals (a cardiologist opining on immunology), and selectively cited fringe researchers are presented as equivalent to — or more credible than — systematic review evidence. The pragma-dialectical analysis shows this as a failure to satisfy the critical questions for the argumentation scheme from expert opinion: the cited authorities are not expert in the relevant domain, or have demonstrable conflicts of interest, or their claims conflict with the overwhelming consensus of in-domain experts. Public health communication that names and corrects the specific critical question failure is more effective than simply asserting "trust the science," because it engages the audience's actual reasoning rather than demanding deference.

> **Software Engineering — Hasty Generalization in Performance Profiling.** Engineers routinely make hasty generalizations from small, unrepresentative profiling samples: "The slowest function in this ten-transaction test is the bottleneck; optimizing it will speed up production." Production workloads may be orders of magnitude larger, have different query distributions, involve cache warming effects, and hit different code paths. The informal logic of the hasty generalization maps directly onto the statistical concept of sampling bias: drawing a universal conclusion from a sample that is too small, too uniform, or systematically unrepresentative. This is a domain where the fallacy has a precise technical correction: rigorous sampling theory and statistical significance testing are the procedural safeguards that prevent the fallacy from propagating into architectural decisions.

> **Everyday Discourse — Equivocation in Economic Debates.** The word "equality" equivocates between at least three distinct concepts: *equality of opportunity* (everyone starts from the same initial conditions), *equality of outcome* (everyone ends with the same resources), and *equality of treatment* (everyone is subject to the same rules). Political arguments routinely shift between these meanings within a single exchange: "I support equality" functions as common ground when the speaker means equal treatment and the listener hears equal opportunity, allowing apparent agreement to mask substantive disagreement. Identifying the equivocation — naming the ambiguity and demanding that the arguer commit to a specific sense — is the single most productive move available to a critical interlocutor in these debates.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Fallacy labeling as refutation** — The most common meta-level error. Attaching the label "ad hominem" or "slippery slope" to an argument does not refute it. It names a pattern that requires scrutiny, not a verdict. The correct response is to identify which specific logical defect is present — which critical question is unanswered, which rule is violated — and then invite repair or defense. Labeling without analysis is the *ad lapidem* move: throwing a stone without explaining why it hits.

- **Treating all informal fallacy instances as fallacious** — Appeal to authority, slippery slope, argument from analogy, and appeal to emotion are argumentation *schemes* with conditions of legitimate use. An appeal to a genuinely qualified, unbiased, in-domain expert is not a fallacy. Emotion that accurately tracks the moral seriousness of a situation is not a fallacy. Reflexively labeling these patterns without applying critical questions produces the opposite problem: dismissing legitimate reasoning as defective.

- **Confusing a fallacy with a weak argument** — Not every bad argument is fallacious. A valid argument with a false premise is not fallacious — it is unsound. An inductive argument from small evidence is not fallacious — it is weak. Fallacies are *patterns* of reasoning that are defective in a specific structural or contextual way, not just any argument we find unconvincing or poorly supported.

- **Begging-the-question vs. "raises the question"** — The contemporary colloquial use of "begging the question" to mean "raises the question" or "invites the question" is a malapropism with costs. The technical meaning — the circular argument structure in which the conclusion is disguised within a premise — identifies a precise and important logical defect. Collapsing the two meanings leaves no clean term for the actual fallacy, degrading the precision that fallacy vocabulary is supposed to provide.

- **Post hoc vs. genuine causal inference** — *Post hoc ergo propter hoc* (after this, therefore because of this) is a genuine fallacy when causation is asserted from mere sequence. But temporal sequence *is* necessary for causation (causes precede effects), so finding that X preceded Y is not fallacious as an initial indicator — it becomes fallacious when treated as *sufficient* evidence. The critical question is whether an independent causal mechanism has been established, not whether temporal sequence has been noted.

- **Ignoring charitable interpretation** — The principle of charity requires interpreting an opponent's argument in its strongest available form before criticizing it. Identifying a fallacy in a weak formulation of an argument, when a stronger formulation is available, is itself a form of straw man. Fallacy identification should always ask: is there a version of this argument that avoids the defect? If so, engage that version.

## Related Concepts

- [[Arguments_Validity_and_Soundness]] — Formal fallacies are precisely the argument forms that violate validity; affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent (covered there) are the two most important formal fallacies in propositional logic; the truth-table method for testing validity is the formal diagnostic tool for formal fallacy identification.

- [[Propositions_and_Truth_Values]] — Equivocation and amphiboly operate by exploiting the gap between the propositional content of a statement and its surface linguistic form; a precise account of how propositions are individuated (same sentence, different proposition when context shifts meaning) is the theoretical basis for ambiguity fallacy analysis.

- [[Argumentation_Theory_and_Dialectic]] — The pragma-dialectical framework (van Eemeren and Grootendorst) defines fallacies as violations of the ten rules of critical discussion; Walton's argumentation schemes with critical questions are the most refined tool for distinguishing legitimate from fallacious uses of each informal fallacy pattern; Dung's abstract argumentation framework models rebuttal and reinstatement.

- [[Classical_Rhetoric_and_Aristotle]] — Aristotle's *De Sophisticis Elenchis* is the founding document of fallacy theory; his thirteen-fallacy catalogue and the in-dictione / extra-dictionem distinction remain foundational; the *Rhetoric* complements this with his account of the enthymeme (the rhetorical syllogism that audiences fill in unconsciously, making suppressed premises — and suppressed fallacies — especially powerful).

- [[Cognitive_Biases]] — Cognitive biases are the psychological mechanisms that make informal fallacies compelling: the availability heuristic makes vivid anecdotal evidence feel more representative than it is (hasty generalization); confirmation bias makes us seek evidence for conclusions we already hold (cherry-picking); the affect heuristic makes emotionally charged premises feel more logically weighty than they are (appeal to emotion). Fallacy theory identifies the logical defect; cognitive science explains why the defect is not automatically visible.

- [[Rhetorical_Figures_and_Tropes]] — Rhetorical figures (anaphora, chiasmus, metaphor) are artful arrangements of language that enhance persuasion; they are not fallacies — they are legitimate tools of effective communication. The boundary between a vivid rhetorical device and a fallacy of ambiguity is crossed when the device exploits multiple meanings to do inferential work covertly. Distinguishing the two requires both rhetorical literacy and fallacy awareness.

- [[Logic_and_Critical_Thinking_Overview]] — The parent overview note establishing the broader framework of deductive, inductive, and abductive reasoning within which fallacy theory is situated; formal fallacies are violations of deductive validity as defined there.

### Foundational

1. Someone argues: "You should not trust Dr. Martinez's research on climate change — she once received funding from an oil company twenty years ago." Reconstruct this as a standard-form argument, identify which informal fallacy pattern it resembles, and then explain what additional information would be needed to determine whether it is actually fallacious or a legitimate critical question about bias.

2. Explain the difference between the *straw man* fallacy and the *principle of charity*. How does applying the principle of charity change your approach to identifying fallacies in an opponent's argument? Give an example where the same argument is fallacious under one interpretation and legitimate under another.

3. Why is "begging the question" not the same as "raising the question"? Construct an example of a circular argument that is subtle enough that most listeners would not immediately notice the circularity, and explain exactly where the circularity lies.

### Undergraduate

1. Aristotle divided his thirteen fallacies into those *in dictione* (language-dependent) and those *extra dictionem* (language-independent). Using three fallacies from each group, explain what this distinction captures and evaluate whether it is a principled theoretical division or a historically contingent convenience. Is modern four-family taxonomy (relevance, presumption, ambiguity, insufficient evidence) a genuine improvement, and in what respect?

2. Van Eemeren and Grootendorst define fallacies as violations of the rules of critical discussion. Walton defines them as misuses of argumentation schemes — argument patterns that are legitimate when their critical questions are satisfied but fallacious when they are not. For the *appeal to authority* and the *slippery slope*, apply both frameworks. Do they give the same verdict? Where they differ, which framework is more useful for practical argument evaluation and why?

3. The *gambler's fallacy* (believing that a coin that has landed heads ten times is "due" for tails) and the *hot-hand fallacy* (believing a basketball player who has made five consecutive shots is "on a streak" and will make the next) are both errors about probability and sequences — yet they point in opposite directions. Are they both the same informal fallacy? If not, what logical distinction separates them, and what does this suggest about the limits of categorical fallacy labeling?

### Advanced

1. Hamblin's 1970 critique argued that the *standard treatment* of fallacies in logic textbooks was incoherent — it never defined what a fallacy was, offered no principled criteria for membership in the catalogue, and silently assumed that fallacies are easy to detect in practice while being theoretically interesting. Evaluate Hamblin's critique in light of subsequent developments in pragma-dialectics and argumentation scheme theory. Has the field answered his objections, or do significant theoretical gaps remain?

2. Relevance logic adds a *relevance condition* to classical validity: not only must the premises necessitate the conclusion, but the premises must be inferentially connected to the conclusion (use its informational content). Consider whether relevance logic provides a formal account of the fallacies of relevance (ad hominem, red herring, appeal to emotion). What can formal relevance logic capture about these fallacies, and where does it fall short? What non-formal resources are needed to complete the account?

3. The pragma-dialectical account defines fallacies as procedural violations of rational discourse. A philosophical critic argues that this makes fallacy theory culturally relative: what counts as a "rule of critical discussion" varies across argumentative cultures, and van Eemeren and Grootendorst's ten rules encode a Northern European liberal-deliberative conception of rational discourse that is not universally shared. Reconstruct the strongest version of this relativist objection and then evaluate what resources pragma-dialectics has for responding to it. Is the response adequate, or must the theory be substantially revised?
